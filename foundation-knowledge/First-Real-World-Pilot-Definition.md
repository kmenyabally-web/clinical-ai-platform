# First Real-World Pilot: Digital CQC Readiness System

**Definition of a Safe, Controlled, and Inspection-Ready Pilot**

*This document defines the first real-world pilot of the digital CQC readiness system in a regulated care setting. It states the purpose, setting, scope, participants, success criteria, risk controls, duration and review, and how to explain the pilot to inspectors. It is not a sales plan or a technical deployment guide. It does not contain code. Plain English only.*

---

## 1. Purpose of the Pilot

### Why a Pilot Is Being Run

- The organisation wants to **test** the system in a real care environment before rolling it out more widely. A pilot allows the organisation to see how staff use the system day to day, whether the folder structure and compliance rules work in practice, and whether anything causes confusion, error, or risk. Running the system only in a controlled pilot first reduces the chance that problems affect many services or many people at once.
- The organisation also wants to **learn** what support staff need (training, guidance, or changes to the system) and what benefits the system actually delivers (for example, easier finding of evidence, clearer view of what is due). Learning in a small pilot is safer and more honest than assuming the system will work everywhere without testing.
- Regulators and the public expect care services to use new systems in a **responsible** way. A defined pilot with clear scope, success criteria, and safeguards shows that the organisation is not rushing; it is testing, learning, and acting on what it finds before scaling.

---

### What the Pilot Is Intended to Prove

- **That the Minimum Viable Live System (MVLS) can be used safely in a live service.** The pilot uses only the features defined in the MVLS: Active Care Folder, document upload and viewing, manual compliance status, reminders and task list, emergency summary, inspection mode, audit trail, service-level view, and review completion and sign-off. The pilot is intended to show that staff can use these features without critical errors, that the folder stays structured and complete, and that no one is harmed or put at risk by the way the system is used.
- **That staff understand their role.** The pilot is intended to show that staff know they must choose where to put documents, complete reviews, and use the emergency summary when needed; that they do not expect the system to make decisions for them; and that they can explain to an internal reviewer (or an inspector) how the system supports them.
- **That the system supports inspection readiness.** The pilot is intended to show that the folder structure matches what inspectors expect, that key documents (care plan, risk assessment, daily notes, medication, consent, incidents, review dates) can be found quickly, and that the service can demonstrate who did what and when via the audit trail. The organisation wants evidence that the system helps rather than hinders when the service is inspected or audited.
- **That issues can be detected and addressed.** The pilot is intended to show that the organisation can spot problems (for example, wrong use, confusion, or gaps in training) and fix them or pause the pilot before rolling out further. So the pilot is also a test of the organisation’s **governance**: can it monitor, learn, and act?

---

### What the Pilot Is NOT Intended to Prove

- **That the system is ready for every type of service.** The pilot runs in one type of setting (e.g. one ward or one team). It is not intended to prove that the system works in every kind of mental health, learning disability, or community service. Other settings may be piloted or rolled out later, with their own assessment.
- **That the system replaces the need for skilled staff or good leadership.** The pilot tests whether the system supports staff and managers. It does not test whether the service would be compliant without capable people. Inspection readiness depends on both the system and the people using it.
- **That AI or automation is safe.** The first pilot uses **only** the MVLS: no AI document classification, no AI gap detection, no automated decisions. The pilot is not intended to prove anything about AI. If the organisation later runs a pilot of AI-assisted features, that will be a separate pilot with its own definition and safeguards.
- **That the system is perfect.** The pilot may reveal things that need to change (training, wording, process, or system design). The purpose is to learn and improve, not to claim that the system is fault-free from day one.

---

## 2. Pilot Setting

### Type of Service

- The pilot runs in **one** of the following (the organisation chooses and records which):
  - **A single mental health ward or unit** (inpatient or similar), or
  - **A single learning disability supported living service** (one house or one small cluster), or
  - **A single community team** (e.g. community mental health team, or a small outreach or home-support team).

The choice depends on where the organisation can provide strong oversight, where staff are willing and able to take part, and where the risk of harm from any pilot problem can be contained. The **same** type of service (e.g. one ward) is used for the whole pilot; the organisation does not switch mid-pilot to a different type.

---

### Scale

- **One service only.** For example: one ward, one supported living house or cluster, or one community team. The pilot does **not** run in multiple wards or multiple teams at the same time. Limiting to one service keeps the pilot manageable and makes it easier to monitor, support, and pause if needed.
- **All people in that service** (or a clearly defined subset). For example, if it is one ward, the pilot may include all people on that ward whose care folder is held in the system. The organisation may define a **maximum number** of people (e.g. no more than 15 or 20) so that the pilot does not become too large to support and review. If the service has more people than the limit, the organisation may choose to include only a defined group (e.g. people under one care co-ordinator) and record why.
- **One team or one shift pattern** where relevant. If the service has multiple teams or shifts, the organisation may choose to involve one team or one set of staff first and expand within the same service later, or to involve all staff who work in the pilot service. The choice is recorded so that everyone knows who is in scope.

---

### Why This Setting Is Appropriate for a First Pilot

- **Contained.** One service (and a bounded number of people) means that if something goes wrong, the impact is limited. The organisation can focus support and monitoring on one place and one group of staff.
- **Real.** The pilot runs in a live service where real care is delivered and where CQC may inspect. So the organisation learns from real use, not from a test environment. Staff use the system for real people and real documents.
- **Oversight.** The organisation chooses a setting where a **registered manager** or equivalent is clearly accountable and where the organisation can stay in close contact (e.g. visits, reviews, feedback). The first pilot should not be in a service that is hard to reach or where governance is weak.
- **Willing.** The service (manager and staff) should be willing to take part and to give honest feedback. A pilot imposed on a reluctant team is less likely to produce useful learning and may create resentment. The organisation should confirm willingness before starting.

---

## 3. Pilot Scope and Limits

### What Parts of the System Will Be Used

- **All features of the Minimum Viable Live System (MVLS):**
  - Active Care Folder (eight sections, document types from the blueprint) for each person in the pilot scope.
  - Document upload and viewing; staff choose section and document type manually when adding a document.
  - Manual compliance status (in date, due, overdue; Green, Amber, Red) as calculated from the rules and the data.
  - Reminders and task list for staff and managers.
  - Emergency summary (seven critical items) for each person.
  - Inspection mode (read-only) for showing the folder and status.
  - Audit trail (who did what, when).
  - Service-level compliance view for the manager.
  - Review completion and sign-off when staff complete a review.

- **No other features.** The pilot uses only what is in the MVLS. No AI, no automated decisions, no features that are still in development or not yet approved for live use.

---

### What Parts Will NOT Be Used

- **No AI-assisted document classification.** During the pilot, staff choose the section and document type from the list themselves. There is no AI suggestion. This keeps the first pilot simple and avoids any risk or confusion from AI until the MVLS has been proven.
- **No AI-assisted gap detection.** Managers and staff see only the rule-based status and task list. There is no AI-generated list of “potential gaps” or AI-written explanations.
- **No automated alerts to people outside the service** (e.g. regulators, families). Any communication outside the service is decided and sent by staff or managers in the usual way.
- **No features that are not part of the approved MVLS.** If the system has optional or future features (e.g. reporting, integrations), they are not used in the pilot unless they are explicitly in the MVLS and in this pilot definition.

---

### Temporary Restrictions Applied During the Pilot

- **Geographic or service restriction:** The system is used **only** in the one pilot service. Other services in the organisation do **not** use the system for live care during the pilot (unless they are already using it under a separate, approved rollout). This avoids mixing “pilot” and “business as usual” in a way that could confuse monitoring or accountability.
- **No “go live” for the rest of the organisation until the pilot is reviewed.** The organisation does not roll out the system to a second service until the pilot has been reviewed and the organisation has decided, on the basis of evidence, that it is safe and appropriate to proceed. The pilot is not a soft launch of a wider rollout; it is a defined test with a clear end and a decision point.
- **Optional: read-only or limited write for certain roles during early weeks.** The organisation may choose that for the **first one or two weeks** of the pilot, only certain roles (e.g. manager and one or two key staff) can enter or edit content, and other staff can view only. This can help the organisation check that the system is set up correctly and that the first entries are right before everyone writes. If used, this restriction is clearly communicated and is lifted after the defined period. This is optional; many pilots may allow all participating staff to use the system fully from the start.

## 4. Participants and Roles

### Which Staff Roles Participate

- **Frontline staff** (support workers, care workers) who deliver care to people in the pilot service and who record daily notes, activities, or other content in the folder.
- **Key workers or care co-ordinators** (or the equivalent role in the pilot service) who are responsible for care plans, risk assessments, and reviews for people in the pilot.
- **Nurses or clinicians** (if the pilot service has them) who record medication, treatment, consent, or other clinical information in the folder.
- **The registered manager** (or equivalent) of the pilot service, who has oversight of the service and who uses the service-level compliance view, audit trail, and inspection mode.
- **A pilot lead or project lead** from the organisation (e.g. compliance lead, quality lead, or designated project manager) who is responsible for monitoring the pilot, collecting feedback, and reporting to the organisation. This person does not need to be in the pilot service every day but must be available and in contact with the manager and staff.

---

### What Each Role Does During the Pilot

- **Frontline staff:** Use the system to add documents (choosing section and type), to enter daily notes or activity records where that is part of their role, and to view the emergency summary when needed. They complete any tasks assigned to them (e.g. “complete daily note”) and may attend training and feedback sessions. They report any problems or confusion to their line manager or the pilot lead.
- **Key workers / care co-ordinators:** Use the system to maintain care plans and risk assessments, to complete reviews and sign-offs, and to keep the folder up to date for their people. They use the task list to see what is due and work with the manager to prioritise. They give feedback on whether the structure and reminders help and whether anything is unclear or time-consuming.
- **Nurses / clinicians:** Use the system to record medication, treatment, consent, or other clinical information in the right sections. They complete reviews for which they are responsible and use the emergency summary when relevant. They report any concerns about clinical safety or record-keeping to the manager and the pilot lead.
- **Registered manager:** Ensures that the pilot runs in line with this definition; that staff are trained and supported; and that the service continues to meet its legal and regulatory duties. They use the service-level view to oversee compliance and to prepare for inspection. They escalate any serious issue (safety, data, or staff concern) to the pilot lead and the organisation immediately. They are the main point of contact for the pilot lead and for any internal or external review of the pilot.
- **Pilot lead:** Coordinates the pilot; ensures training and materials are in place; collects feedback and usage information (as agreed); and reports to the organisation on progress, issues, and learning. They do not make clinical or care decisions; they support the manager and staff and help the organisation decide whether to continue, change, or stop the pilot.

---

### Who Has Oversight and Accountability

- **Service level:** The **registered manager** of the pilot service is accountable for the safe running of the service during the pilot. They are accountable for ensuring that care is not compromised by the pilot and that staff use the system in line with policy and training. If the pilot causes risk or confusion, the manager must act (including pausing or escalating) and is supported by the organisation to do so.
- **Organisation level:** A **senior manager**, **compliance lead**, or **governance committee** (the organisation decides and records who) is accountable for **approving** the pilot, for **reviewing** it at the end (or at defined points), and for **deciding** whether to continue, extend, or roll out further. This role ensures that the pilot definition is followed, that evidence is collected, and that the decision to move beyond the pilot is based on that evidence and on the exit criteria from the MVLS and the Controlled Technical Integration Plan.

---

## 5. Success Criteria

The pilot is considered **successful** if the following are met. They are the basis for the organisation’s review and for the decision on whether to proceed.

---

### Improved Visibility of Documentation

- **Evidence:** Staff and the manager can find key documents (care plan, risk assessment, consent, incidents, review dates) in the system without long searching. They can see what is in date, due, or overdue. In the review, the organisation may ask: “Can you show us the care plan for [person]?” and “Can you show us what is overdue this month?” and expect a quick, clear answer. Feedback from staff and manager supports that the structure and status help them see what they have and what they need to do.
- **Why it matters:** If the system does not improve visibility, it is not adding value. The pilot should show that the folder structure and compliance status make it easier to see the state of documentation than before (e.g. than paper or scattered records).

---

### Reduced Inspection Preparation Stress (or No Increase)

- **Evidence:** The manager (and staff, if asked) report that preparing for inspection or internal audit is **no harder** than before, and ideally **easier**, because the folder is in one place, the structure is clear, and the service-level view shows what might need attention. There is no evidence that the system has made preparation more stressful or more chaotic.
- **Why it matters:** The system is intended to support inspection readiness. If it increases stress or confusion, that is a negative outcome. The pilot should not make things worse.

---

### No Safety Incidents Related to System Use

- **Evidence:** No incident (harm to a person, near-miss, or serious concern) is caused by or clearly linked to the use of the system during the pilot. For example: no wrong information in the folder that led to wrong care; no missed emergency information because it was in the wrong place or not visible; no breach of confidentiality or data safety that harmed a person or the service. If any incident occurs that might be related to the system, it is reported, investigated, and recorded; the pilot may be paused until the cause is understood.
- **Why it matters:** Safety is non-negotiable. The pilot must not harm anyone. This criterion is a **must**: if there is a safety incident related to system use, the pilot cannot be declared successful without a full review and, where appropriate, changes or pause.

---

### Staff Can Use the System as Intended

- **Evidence:** Staff complete reviews and record them in the system; they add documents and choose section and type; they use the emergency summary when relevant; they do not routinely bypass the system or use it in a way that contradicts training (e.g. they do not leave documents “unfiled” or skip the sign-off step). Spot checks or sampling by the manager or pilot lead support that use is consistent with the MVLS design. Feedback shows that staff understand their role (they decide; the system supports) and do not expect the system to make decisions for them.
- **Why it matters:** If staff cannot or do not use the system as intended, the system is not viable. The pilot should show that with training and support, staff can use it correctly.

---

### Audit Trail and Accountability Are Clear

- **Evidence:** When asked “who reviewed this care plan?” or “when was this document added?”, the service can answer using the audit trail in the system. The trail is complete enough to support accountability and to show an inspector or internal auditor that humans are making the decisions and that changes are traceable.
- **Why it matters:** Inspection readiness and governance depend on being able to show who did what and when. The pilot should confirm that the system supports that.

---

### Summary: Success Criteria

| Criterion | What success looks like |
|-----------|---------------------------|
| **Improved visibility** | Key documents and status are easy to find; staff and manager confirm the system helps. |
| **No increase in inspection stress** | Preparation is no harder, ideally easier; no evidence of added chaos. |
| **No safety incidents** | No harm or serious concern caused by or linked to system use; any incident is reported and reviewed. |
| **Staff use as intended** | Reviews and documents are recorded correctly; staff understand their role; no routine misuse. |
| **Audit and accountability** | “Who did what, when?” can be answered from the system; trail supports inspection and governance. |

---

## 6. Risk Controls and Safeguards

### How Risks Are Monitored

- **Regular contact:** The pilot lead (or designated person) stays in contact with the registered manager and, where appropriate, with staff. Contact may be weekly or as agreed (e.g. twice weekly in the first two weeks, then weekly). The purpose is to hear about problems early: confusion, errors, workload concerns, or anything that might affect safety or quality.
- **Usage and behaviour:** The organisation may collect simple, agreed measures (e.g. how many documents were added, how many reviews were completed, whether status is being updated) to see if the system is being used. This is not surveillance of individuals; it is checking that the pilot is active and that there are no obvious patterns of wrong use (e.g. no reviews completed, or many documents in the wrong section). The organisation should be clear with staff about what is collected and why.
- **Spot checks:** The manager or pilot lead may occasionally look at a sample of folders (e.g. one or two people) to check that documents are in the right place, that reviews are recorded, and that the emergency summary is populated. This helps detect drift or misunderstanding without waiting for an incident.
- **Open feedback:** Staff and the manager are encouraged to report anything that worries them: “I’m not sure where to put this,” “This took too long,” “We had a near-miss because we couldn’t find X.” Feedback is logged and acted on (training, guidance, or system change) so that risks are addressed before they become incidents.

---

### How Issues Are Reported

- **To the manager:** Staff report day-to-day issues (confusion, errors, suggestions) to their line manager or the registered manager. The manager decides whether to fix locally (e.g. extra training) or to escalate to the pilot lead.
- **To the pilot lead:** The manager (or staff, if the organisation allows) reports issues to the pilot lead. The pilot lead logs them and decides whether to: (a) resolve with training or guidance, (b) refer to the organisation for a system or process change, or (c) treat as serious and consider pausing the pilot.
- **Serious issues:** Any issue that could affect **safety** (e.g. wrong information in the folder, emergency information missing or wrong, breach of confidentiality), **regulatory compliance** (e.g. evidence that could mislead an inspector), or **staff or person wellbeing** (e.g. bullying, overwhelming workload linked to the system) must be reported to the pilot lead **and** to the organisation’s accountable role **immediately**. The organisation may define “immediately” (e.g. within 24 hours or same day). Serious issues are recorded and may trigger a pause or stop of the pilot.

---

### How the Pilot Can Be Paused or Stopped Immediately

- **Authority to pause:** The **registered manager** of the pilot service has the authority to **pause** the pilot with immediate effect if they believe that continuing would put people at risk or would breach their professional or legal duties. Pause means: stop using the system for new entries or for day-to-day care folder use; the service may revert to the previous way of holding records (e.g. paper or existing system) until the organisation has reviewed and decided. The manager must inform the pilot lead and the organisation as soon as they pause and must record the reason.
- **Authority to stop:** The **organisation’s accountable role** (senior manager, compliance lead, or governance committee) has the authority to **stop** the pilot at any time. Stop means: the pilot is ended; the system may be withdrawn from the pilot service or kept in read-only mode for reference while the organisation decides next steps. The reason is recorded and participants are informed.
- **Triggers for pause or stop:** The organisation should define triggers in advance, for example: (a) a safety incident caused by or clearly linked to the system; (b) a data breach or serious confidentiality concern; (c) repeated or serious wrong use that cannot be corrected quickly; (d) regulator direction or serious regulator concern; (e) the manager or staff losing confidence that they can use the system safely. When a trigger is met, the manager or the organisation acts without delay. There is no requirement to “wait and see”; caution comes first.
- **After pause or stop:** The organisation reviews what happened, supports the service to return to a safe way of working (e.g. previous record-keeping), and decides whether to fix and restart the pilot, to change the pilot definition, or to stop the pilot and not proceed with the system in that form. The decision and the reason are recorded.

---

## 7. Pilot Duration and Review

### Expected Duration of the Pilot

- The pilot runs for a **defined period** (e.g. **8 to 12 weeks** from the date the pilot service starts using the system for live care). The organisation sets the exact length and records it (e.g. “Pilot runs from [date] to [date]”).
- The duration is long enough to allow **real use** (several review cycles, various types of documents, and different staff using the system) and **reliable feedback**, but not so long that the pilot drifts into “business as usual” without a formal review. Eight to twelve weeks is a common range for a first pilot; the organisation may choose a shorter period (e.g. 6 weeks) if the service is small and use is intensive, or a longer period (e.g. 14 weeks) if the organisation wants more evidence. The choice is documented.
- **Extension:** If the organisation needs more time to collect evidence or to address issues without stopping the pilot, it may **extend** the pilot by a defined period (e.g. 4 more weeks). The reason for extension is recorded (e.g. “To allow completion of care plan reviews for all people in scope” or “To retrain staff after early confusion”). Extension is not automatic; it is a deliberate decision by the accountable role.

---

### How and When It Will Be Reviewed

- **Mid-pilot check (optional):** Around the **midpoint** of the pilot (e.g. week 4 or 5 of an 8–12 week pilot), the pilot lead and the manager (and optionally a small group of staff) hold a **short review**: what is working, what is not, what has been learned, and whether any issues need immediate action. The outcome is recorded (e.g. “No major issues; continue with focus on X” or “Issue Y identified; action Z taken”). This is not a full success assessment; it is a check to catch problems early.
- **End-of-pilot review:** At the **end** of the defined pilot period, the organisation holds a **formal review**. The review considers: (1) the success criteria (visibility, inspection stress, safety, staff use, audit trail); (2) the evidence collected (feedback, usage, spot checks, any incidents); (3) whether the pilot should be declared successful, partially successful, or unsuccessful; and (4) whether the organisation should proceed to a wider rollout, run a second pilot (e.g. in a different type of service), or stop. The review is led by the accountable role and may involve the pilot lead, the manager, and others as needed. The **decision** and **reason** are recorded and communicated to participants and to anyone who needs to know (e.g. senior leadership, board).

---

### What Evidence Will Be Collected

- **Feedback from staff and manager:** Structured or informal feedback on: ease of use, clarity of structure, whether the system helps with finding documents and seeing what is due, any problems or confusion, and whether they would recommend continuing. Feedback may be collected via short surveys, interviews, or group discussions. Participation is voluntary; the organisation is clear about how feedback will be used (pilot review, improvement) and who will see it.
- **Usage evidence:** As agreed with the organisation and in line with data protection: how many documents were added, how many reviews were completed, whether the emergency summary is populated for people in scope, and whether the audit trail shows who did what and when. This is aggregated or anonymised where possible; the purpose is to see that the system is being used, not to judge individual performance.
- **Spot checks or sampling:** Notes from any spot checks (e.g. “Sample of 3 folders checked; documents in correct sections; reviews recorded”) to support the “staff use as intended” and “audit trail” success criteria.
- **Incidents and issues:** A log of any incidents, near-misses, or serious issues reported during the pilot, with the outcome (e.g. “Resolved with training,” “Pilot paused,” “No further action”). This supports the “no safety incidents” criterion and the organisation’s ability to show that it monitored and acted.
- **Inspection or audit experience (if applicable):** If the pilot service is inspected or internally audited during the pilot, a short note on whether the system helped or hindered (e.g. “Inspector was able to view folder in inspection mode; no negative feedback”). This is optional (inspection may not happen during the pilot) but valuable if available.

## 8. Inspection-Safe Explanation

The following explanation may be **given to a CQC inspector** if they ask about the pilot. It is honest and emphasises caution, learning, and safety.

---

**Our First Pilot of the Digital CQC Readiness System**

We are running a **first pilot** of a digital system that holds our care folders and helps us track what’s in date and what’s due for review. We’re doing it in a **controlled way** so we can learn and make sure it’s safe before we use it more widely.

**What we’re doing**

- The pilot runs in **one service only** [e.g. one ward / one supported living service / one community team] for a **set period** [e.g. 8–12 weeks]. We’re not rolling it out everywhere yet.
- We’re using only the **core features**: the care folder with the same structure as the CQC blueprint, document storage, compliance status from our rules and data, reminders, emergency summary, and a read-only inspection view. We’re **not** using any AI or automated decisions in this pilot. Staff choose where to put documents and complete reviews themselves; the system supports them, it doesn’t decide for them.
- Our staff and manager have been **trained** and we’re **monitoring** how it goes. We collect feedback and we’ve set **success criteria** (for example, that the system helps with visibility and doesn’t cause safety incidents). At the end of the pilot we’ll **review** the evidence and decide whether to continue, change anything, or roll out further.

**Why we’re doing it this way**

- We want to **test** the system in a real service before we rely on it more widely. That way we can fix any problems in one place and learn what support staff need. We’re not assuming it will work everywhere without testing.
- **Safety first.** If our manager or our organisation has any serious concern during the pilot, we can **pause or stop** it straight away. We’re not committed to continuing regardless; we’re committed to learning and acting on what we find.
- If you’re inspecting our pilot service, you’ll see the same folder structure and evidence you’d expect. The pilot doesn’t change what we record or how we’re accountable; it just changes how we **store and view** it. We’re happy to show you the system and to explain how we’re running the pilot if that would be helpful.
